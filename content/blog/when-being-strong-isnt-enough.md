There was a time when I would have told you that I was mentally strong.

In fact, I believed it for most of my adult life.

I was the person people came to when they needed to vent. When someone was having a terrible day, needed advice, or simply needed someone to listen, I was usually there.

I didn't mind being that person.

I actually took pride in it.

I learned to joke around, make people laugh, and keep things moving when the atmosphere around me became negative. Humor became one of the ways I protected myself from absorbing everyone else's frustration.

If someone was angry, I tried to make them laugh.

If someone was discouraged, I tried to lift them up.

If someone needed to complain, I listened.

I thought that was resilience.

And maybe, in some ways, it was.

But I eventually learned something important:

**Being mentally strong does not mean you are immune to stress.**

## When Work Stress Follows You Home

For years, I believed in loyalty.

I was raised with the idea that you found a job, worked hard, stayed loyal, built a career, and eventually retired.

Twenty years with the same company didn't sound strange to me.

It sounded like success.

It meant commitment.

It meant stability.

It meant you had earned your place.

But the workplace has changed.

Today, careers can be much more fluid. Managers move from position to position. Employees change departments. Companies restructure. People compete for promotions. Leadership changes can happen quickly.

In one part of my career, supervisors and managers seemed to rotate every year to a year and a half.

Just as you learned how one person operated—what they expected, how they communicated, what they valued, and sometimes what they did not tolerate—you would find yourself starting over with someone new.

Then the process repeated.

Learn the new person.

Adapt.

Adjust.

Try to figure out what works.

Deal with the things that don't.

Then someone else arrives.

That constant uncertainty can become exhausting.

Workplace stress isn't simply about having a difficult day. Occupational stress can involve the psychological and physiological response to workplace conditions, including workload, lack of control, job insecurity, and relationships with supervisors and coworkers.

And sometimes the hardest part isn't what happens at work.

It's what happens **after work**.

## The Problem With Dwelling

Have you ever left work physically, but mentally you were still sitting at your desk?

You replay the conversation.

You think about what someone said.

You think about what you should have said.

You wonder whether you handled something correctly.

You get angry all over again.

Then you replay it again tomorrow.

This is where stress can become especially difficult.

The stressful event may be over, but your mind continues responding to it.

Psychologists sometimes refer to this type of repetitive negative thinking as **rumination**—repeatedly focusing on distressing thoughts or experiences without reaching a productive solution.

And there is an important distinction:

**Thinking about a problem to solve it can be productive. Thinking about the same problem repeatedly without being able to change it can become destructive.**

Chronic stress can keep the body's stress-response system activated for too long. Stress hormones such as cortisol and adrenaline are useful when we need to respond to an immediate threat, but prolonged activation can affect multiple systems throughout the body.

That can show up as:

- Trouble sleeping
- Fatigue
- Headaches
- Muscle tension
- Digestive problems
- Irritability
- Difficulty concentrating
- Anxiety
- Changes in appetite
- Feeling emotionally exhausted

Long-term stress can contribute to worsening health problems and can affect sleep, concentration, mood, appetite, energy, and physical symptoms.

## The Strong Person Can Still Get Tired

This is something I wish more people understood.

Sometimes the people who appear the strongest are carrying more than anyone realizes.

They are the ones everyone calls.

The ones who listen.

The ones who make jokes when everyone else is frustrated.

The ones who say, "I'm good."

The ones who don't want to burden anyone else with their problems.

But constantly absorbing other people's emotions can become exhausting.

You can develop what might be described as **emotional overload**—a point where the constant demands on your emotional resources begin to exceed your ability to recover.

And when that happens, simply telling yourself to "toughen up" isn't necessarily resilience.

Sometimes resilience means recognizing that something is hurting you and doing something about it.

## Your Body Doesn't Know It's "Just Work"

One of the biggest lessons I've learned is that your body doesn't necessarily understand the difference between a physical threat and a prolonged psychological stressor.

Your nervous system responds to perceived threats.

That means an unhealthy workplace can create a physical stress response even when nothing physically dangerous is happening.

Your heart rate can increase.

Your muscles can tense.

Your sleep can suffer.

Your stomach can react.

Your mind can become hyperfocused on the next problem.

Over time, repeated stress can create what researchers describe as **allostatic load**—the cumulative physiological burden associated with repeated adaptation to stress.

That doesn't mean every stressful job will make someone sick.

It does mean that chronic stress deserves to be taken seriously.

## So What Can We Actually Do?

We can't always control our workplace.

We can't control every manager.

We can't control whether someone else behaves professionally.

And we certainly can't control every change that happens in our careers.

But there are things we can control.

### 1. Recognize the Stress

The first step is admitting that you're stressed.

Don't wait until your body forces you to acknowledge it.

Pay attention to changes in:

- Sleep
- Mood
- Appetite
- Concentration
- Energy
- Patience
- Relationships

Tracking your stressors and your reactions to them can help identify patterns and recognize which situations are creating the greatest strain.

### 2. Separate Problems From Thoughts

Ask yourself:

**"Is there something I can actually do about this?"**

If the answer is yes, identify the next action.

If the answer is no, continuing to replay the situation may not help.

Sometimes the healthiest response is:

**"This happened. I can't change it. What can I control now?"**

That isn't giving up.

That's redirecting your energy.

### 3. Give Your Brain an Off Switch

Create a transition between work and home.

Take a walk.

Exercise.

Listen to music.

Spend time with family.

Work on a hobby.

Sit outside.

Read.

Laugh.

Do something that reminds your brain that your identity is bigger than your job.

Physical activity, relaxation techniques, journaling, spending time outdoors, and maintaining social connections can all be useful ways to manage stress.

### 4. Protect Your Sleep

Sleep isn't a luxury.

It's part of your health.

If you're lying in bed replaying workplace conversations every night, your body may be resting while your mind is still working.

Create a routine that allows your brain to slow down.

Reduce stimulating activities before bed.

Keep a consistent sleep schedule.

And if persistent sleep problems are affecting your life, talk with a healthcare professional.

### 5. Talk to Someone

Strong people need support too.

Talk to a friend.

Talk to someone you trust.

Talk to a counselor.

Use an Employee Assistance Program if your employer provides one.

And if stress, anxiety, sadness, or other symptoms are interfering with your daily life, consider speaking with a licensed mental-health professional.

Asking for help isn't weakness.

**It's maintenance.**

### 6. Set Boundaries

You don't have to carry everyone else's problems.

You can care about people without becoming responsible for their emotions.

You can listen without absorbing everything.

You can help someone without destroying yourself in the process.

Healthy boundaries aren't selfish.

They are protective.

Creating work-life boundaries and making time to recharge can help reduce the effects of chronic workplace stress.

## Maybe Mental Strength Looks Different Than I Thought

For a long time, I thought mental strength meant being able to handle anything.

Now I think it means something different.

Mental strength isn't pretending nothing bothers you.

It isn't being the person everyone dumps their problems on while you quietly carry your own.

It isn't staying in an unhealthy situation simply because you promised yourself you would be loyal.

And it isn't refusing help because you believe you should be strong enough to handle everything alone.

Maybe mental strength is recognizing when something isn't healthy.

Maybe it's knowing when to step back.

Maybe it's setting boundaries.

Maybe it's asking for help.

Maybe it's changing direction.

And sometimes, maybe it's simply admitting:

**"I'm not okay right now, and that's okay. I need to take care of myself."**

## Your Career Is Part of Your Life — Not Your Entire Life

Work matters.

Providing for your family matters.

Building a career matters.

Loyalty matters.

But none of those things should require you to completely sacrifice your mental and physical health.

The workplace can change.

Managers can change.

Companies can change.

Your career can change.

But you only get one mind and one body.

Take care of both.

And if you are the person everyone else leans on, remember this:

**You deserve somewhere to lean too.**
